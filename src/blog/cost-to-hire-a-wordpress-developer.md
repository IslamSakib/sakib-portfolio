You've got a WordPress job and you're asking what it should cost. The cost to hire a WordPress developer ranges from under $20 to over $200 an hour. The spread is real, and price alone tells you little. What you're really buying is how often the work has to be redone. Here's what the market charges in 2026 and how to tell which end of it you need.

## What do WordPress developers charge per hour in 2026?

These ranges come from pages I checked on 15 October 2026. Marketplaces and agencies publish most rate guides, so treat them as ranges, not facts.

- **Freelancers by experience (worldwide):** about $25 to $50 an hour for junior developers, $50 to $95 for mid-level and $95 to $160 for senior ([goLance's 2026 rate guide](https://golance.com/hiring/best-freelance-wordpress-developers-hourly-rate))
- **Vetted WordPress platform:** $80 to $120 an hour on Codeable, priced by scope, complexity and urgency ([Codeable's help centre](https://help.codeable.io/en/articles/368550-can-codeable-help-with-my-project))
- **Agencies:** roughly $90 to $250 an hour and more for specialist agencies ([Qualimero](https://qualimero.com/en/blog/woocommerce-developer))

## What will my project probably cost?

Project prices depend on the number of unique templates and custom features (forms, calculators, payments, integrations), not on the page count. A small marketing site built from a finished design sits at the low end. A store with custom checkout logic or a custom plugin costs several times more. Ask for a quote that lists templates and features separately, so you can see what drives the price.

## Why does the cheapest quote often cost the most?

The real cost of a WordPress build shows up after launch: the plugin conflict at the first update, the slow pages that cost you rankings, the developer who stops replying. A higher hourly rate that ships clean, fast code once is usually cheaper over a year than a low rate you pay twice.

## How do I judge a developer before I pay them?

You can do it in an hour, before any money changes hands:

1. **Look at live sites, not screenshots.** Open their work on your phone and run it through Google PageSpeed Insights.
2. **Ask what went wrong on a past project and how they fixed it.** Good developers have specific answers.
3. **Ask who owns the code and the logins.** It should be you.
4. **Ask what happens after launch.** Updates, fixes and changes should be clear before you start.
5. **Start with a small paid task.** It tells you more than any portfolio.

## Should I pay hourly, a fixed price or a retainer?

How you pay matters as much as the rate:

- **Hourly** suits small fixes, investigations and work where nobody can know the scope up front, such as tracking down why a site keeps crashing. Ask for a cap so a small job can't quietly become a large bill.
- **Fixed price** suits builds and redesigns with a clear scope: a page list, a design and agreed features. You know the cost in advance. The developer carries the risk of underestimating, so expect the quote to include a margin for that.
- **Monthly retainer** suits ongoing work: maintenance, regular changes or a developer on call for an agency. You pay for reserved time, which is usually cheaper per hour than ad hoc work.

Many good engagements combine them: a fixed price for the build, then a monthly plan to look after it.

## Freelancer, agency or platform: which suits me?

I compare the three in more depth in [freelance WordPress developer vs agency](/blog/freelance-wordpress-developer-vs-agency/). Here's the short version.

**A freelancer** is usually the best value for small and mid-sized businesses: you talk to the person doing the work, and you pay for one person instead of an office. The risk is availability, so ask how they handle holidays and urgent problems.

**An agency** brings designers, project managers and several developers. It's worth it for large projects that need many skills at once, but you pay for that structure even when you only need one developer.

**A vetted platform** sits in between: a marketplace that screens developers and handles payment. Rates are higher than open freelance sites, in exchange for the screening.

## What red flags should I look for in a quote?

Be careful with a quote that:

- Has no written scope, only a price
- Promises page-one Google rankings or a guaranteed traffic number
- Plans to install a premium theme or plugin from an unofficial source (pirated "nulled" plugins are a common source of malware)
- Doesn't say who owns the code, the hosting account and the domain
- Leaves out testing, launch and what happens when something breaks after launch

A good quote answers all of these without being asked.

## What should I own when the work is done?

Whoever you hire, you should end up with full ownership: administrator access to WordPress, the hosting account in your name, the domain registered to you, licences for any premium plugins in your name, and the files and database. If a developer insists on keeping any of these, you're renting your own website.

## What will I keep paying after the site is built?

The build price isn't the whole cost of a WordPress site. Budget for:

- **Hosting:** from a few dollars a month for a small brochure site to much more for a busy store. Cheap hosting is often the reason a site is slow later.
- **Domain and email:** a yearly domain fee, and email hosting if you don't already have it.
- **Premium plugin and theme licences:** many renew yearly. A page builder, a forms plugin and a backup tool can add up.
- **Maintenance:** updates, backups and monitoring, either your own time or a monthly plan. See [WordPress maintenance cost](/blog/wordpress-maintenance-cost/) for typical prices.
- **Changes:** new pages, features and content edits as the business grows.

Ask any developer for a rough yearly running cost along with the build quote. A cheaper build on expensive or fragile foundations can cost more within a year than a better build would have.

## Where do I find a WordPress developer I can trust?

Referrals from people whose sites you like are the most reliable route. After that, look at developers who show live client sites with named results, publish their prices, and write clearly about the problems they solve. Open marketplaces give you the widest choice and the widest range of quality, so check the work before the price. Whoever you shortlist, a small paid first task tells you more than any portfolio.
