You've got a signed project and no free developer, so you're looking for a white label WordPress partner. A white label partner builds and fixes sites under your agency's name, and choosing one is about process more than code. Agency owners who outsource WordPress builds tend to say the same thing: the code was rarely the problem. The problems were missed dates, slow replies, sites that broke tracking or loaded slowly, and the occasional partner who contacted the client directly. With WordPress running [39.7% of all websites](https://w3techs.com/technologies/details/cm-wordpress), most agencies sell it. Here's what to check before you trust one with a client.

## What does white label actually mean for your agency?

A white label WordPress partner builds, fixes or maintains sites that you deliver under your own brand. Your client deals only with you. The partner never appears in emails, code comments, credits or invoices to the client, and usually works under an NDA.

## What should I check before I send the first project?

Each of these is a place outsourced projects tend to go wrong.

**Communication.** Who will you actually talk to: the developer, or an account manager passing messages along? How fast do they reply, and do their working hours overlap with yours? A partner in a different time zone can work well if there's a daily overlap.

**Speed of the finished sites.** Ask for live sites they built and run them through Google PageSpeed Insights on mobile. A slow site reflects on your agency, not on them.

**How they handle your designs.** Do they build what's in the Figma file, at every screen size, or approximate it? Ask how they handle states the design didn't draw, such as error messages and empty states.

**The editing experience.** Your client will edit the site after launch. Ask whether they build with the block editor, ACF fields or a page builder, and why.

**Handoff.** What do you get at the end: logins, a list of plugins and why each is there, notes on how to edit each template?

**Confidentiality.** Will they sign your NDA? Will their name appear anywhere on the site? The answer to the second should be no.

## What do I ask a partner on the first call?

Listen for specific answers, not reassurance.

1. Can I see two live sites you built from someone else's design?
2. What happens if a deadline moves, on either side?
3. How many rounds of changes are included, and what does a change cost after that?
4. Who supports the site after launch, and on what terms?
5. Can we start with one small paid task?

## What warning signs mean a partner will let me down?

Slow down, or walk away, if you see any of these:

- No live examples, only screenshots
- Prices that seem too good to include testing and quality checks
- Vague answers about who does the work
- No written scope or delivery date before work starts

## Should I hire a freelancer or a white-label agency?

White-label partners come in two shapes. A white-label agency has a team, a project manager and set processes. That helps when you need many developers at once or round-the-clock cover. A freelance white-label developer is one senior person you talk to directly. They're usually faster to brief, cheaper, and more consistent, because the same person does every job.

If you send a steady flow of WordPress builds and fixes, a freelancer you trust usually covers it. Larger agencies sometimes use both: a freelancer for builds that need care and an agency for volume.

## How will a white label partner price my work?

Partners usually price in one of a few ways, and each one changes your margin:

- **Per project:** a fixed price per site or per template. Predictable, and good for builds from a finished design.
- **Hourly:** for fixes, changes and work on existing client sites where the scope is unclear.
- **Monthly block of hours:** reserved capacity each month at a lower rate. Good once you know your volume.
- **Resold care plans:** the partner maintains your clients' sites and you bill the client under your brand, keeping the margin.

Ask how unused hours, rush jobs and changes after sign-off are charged. Those three are where budgets drift.

## How do I keep my client relationship safe?

The point of white label is that your client sees only you. Make that explicit:

- An NDA before you share client files
- No contact with your client, in any channel, unless you ask for it
- Nothing in the code, comments, admin users or emails that carries the partner's name
- Work done in your tools: your Slack, your project board, your staging environment if you have one
- Handover notes written so your team can support the site afterwards

A good partner offers all of this before you ask.

## How do I know the partnership is working?

After the first few projects, look at three things: how many rounds of review each build needed, whether deadlines were met without chasing, and how often your client found a bug after launch. A partner who gets better on all three over time is worth keeping. One who needs the same feedback repeated is costing you more than their rate suggests.

## What goes in my first brief?

A partner can only be as good as the brief. For the first project, send:

- The Figma file (or the design in whatever tool you use), with mobile frames
- The page list and which template each page uses
- How your client will edit the site afterwards: blocks, a page builder or fixed templates
- Integrations: forms, CRM, newsletter, analytics, booking or payments
- The deadline, and any dates in between, such as a client review
- Where the site will be hosted and who owns the hosting account
- Your quality bar: browsers, devices and speed targets you check before handing a site to a client

A partner who reads this and comes back with questions before quoting is a good sign. One who quotes instantly on a vague brief has usually priced in the guesswork, and the guesses show up at review.

## Why start with one small paid task?

It's the safest way to choose a partner. One small paid job, such as a single page build or a fix on a client site, shows you how they communicate, how they handle feedback and how clean the result is, before you trust them with a launch.
