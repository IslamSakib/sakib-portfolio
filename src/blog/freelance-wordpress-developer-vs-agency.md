A freelance WordPress developer is often the best fit when your site makes money, you don't have a developer in-house, and you want one accountable person who learns your site once. An agency fits when you need a whole team. A care plan company fits when you mostly need looking after, not building. Here's how to tell which one you need, with the trade-offs nobody puts on their sales page.

## Which one do you need? A quick answer

| Your situation | Usually the best fit |
|---|---|
| Your site is slow, broken or your last developer vanished | A senior freelance WordPress developer |
| You need a new brand, design, content and site in one go | An agency |
| Your site works; you just want updates, backups and someone on call | A care plan company, or a freelancer's care plan |
| You're an agency with more projects than developers | A white-label freelancer |
| You need cover every hour of every day | An agency or a large care plan company |

## Hiring a freelance WordPress developer: what you actually get

You talk to the person who writes the code. Nothing gets lost between a sales rep, a project manager and a developer you never meet. A good senior freelancer reads your site once, remembers it, and fixes the cause instead of adding another plugin.

**What it costs you:** freelance rates run from under $20 to well over $100 an hour, depending on experience and where they're based. The cheapest quote is rarely the cheapest job, because rework is where the money goes. My breakdown of [what it costs to hire a WordPress developer](/blog/cost-to-hire-a-wordpress-developer/) goes through real rates.

**The risk:** one person is one point of failure. If they get ill or disappear, you need to be able to carry on. That's why you should insist on three things from day one: you own every login, you get written notes after each job, and backups sit somewhere you control.

## Hiring an agency: when a team is worth paying for

An agency makes sense when your project needs several people at once: a brand designer, a copywriter, a developer and someone running ads. You get one contract and a project manager who holds it together.

**What it costs you:** more per hour, because you're paying for the team around the developer too. Published price guides put agency maintenance plans at about $200 to over $1,000 a month (see [what WordPress maintenance costs](/blog/wordpress-maintenance-cost/)).

**What to check:** who will actually work on your site, whether the developer is in-house or outsourced, and what happens when your project manager leaves.

## Signing up with a care plan company

Care plan companies sell maintenance as a product: a fixed monthly price for updates, backups, monitoring and a set amount of edits. Prices for published plans run roughly $59 to $799 a month; I compared five of them in [the best WordPress maintenance services](/blog/best-wordpress-maintenance-services/).

**Good for:** sites that already work and need steady care. **Not built for:** a site that's broken in ways nobody understands yet, or a rebuild. Most plans quote that work separately.

## The questions that decide it

1. **Is your site working today?** If not, start with a developer who will find the cause, freelance or agency. A care plan won't fix what's already broken.
2. **Do you need one skill or five?** One skill (WordPress) points to a freelancer; five points to an agency.
3. **How fast do you need a reply, and at what hours?** A solo freelancer can promise a reply within a working day, not someone awake at 3am every night.
4. **Who will know your site in a year?** The same freelancer usually will. At an agency or care company, ask how often your developer changes.
5. **Do you own everything?** Whoever you pick, you should hold every login, the code and the backups.

## How to hire a freelancer without taking a gamble

- **Start with one small paid job.** A fix or a speed check shows you how they work before you commit.
- **Get a written quote first.** It should say what will be done, what it costs and how long it takes.
- **Ask how they test changes.** "On a copy of your site before it goes live" is the answer you want.
- **Check the proof.** Real sites, real numbers and people you could contact, not a template portfolio.
- **Agree the handover.** Notes on what changed, so the next person isn't starting from zero.
